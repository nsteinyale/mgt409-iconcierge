2. Motivation

Most of life's expensive decisions land on families who have no professional to call. Buying a first home, deciding whether to pay off a credit card or start a Roth IRA, reading a lease before signing it, or choosing life insurance when a baby is coming: each question crosses several fields at once (budget, taxes, contracts, insurance), and each field has its own expert who bills by the hour.

  • Most people go without financial advice. About one in three U.S. adults (34%) works with a financial advisor, versus 74% of millionaires (Northwestern Mutual 2025 Planning & Progress Study, as reported by 24/7 Wall St.).
  • Most legal problems get no help. Low-income Americans received no or not enough legal help for 92% of their substantial civil legal problems (Legal Services Corporation, 2022 Justice Gap Study).
  • Help is expensive. The average U.S. lawyer's hourly rate is $349 (Clio Legal Trends).

The idea grew out of earlier work on housing navigation in New Haven: people in a housing crisis rarely have just one problem. A missed rent payment is also a budget problem, a benefits problem and sometimes a legal problem. A single chatbot answers whatever it's asked; a team can notice that a question about a down payment is also a question about an emergency fund and life insurance.

Goal. Give any household the experience of a family office ("let me get the right people on it") for less than a penny per question, without telling anyone what to do: options, numbers, trade-offs and sources, and the family decides.

It is deliberately not a "chat with your PDF" tool: documents are one input the specialists can choose to read, not the product. Compared with resource directories such as New Haven Navigator, iConcierge doesn't send people to a list; it works the problem with them and shows its math.

4. The agents

Roles and tools

AgentRoleTools it may call
🧭 Captain (family-chosen name, look, style)Reads the profile, history and documents list; plans; assigns 0–3 specialists; declines harmful requests; writes the final answer, sources and to-dosNone (it delegates). Output is JSON.
⚖️ Ava, AttorneyLeases, contracts, consumer rights, wills, free legal helpOfficial-source search, family profile, list/read documents

🏠 Leo, Housing & LifestyleRent vs. buy, mortgages, affordability+ mortgage, affordability, rent vs. buy, budget

Permissions are enforced in code (tools.TOOLSETS): a specialist that calls another specialist's tool gets an error, not a result. No agent has internet, email or payment tools.

How they are organized

  1. Input guard cleans the message (Section 6).
  2. Captain plans (JSON): acknowledgment, 0–3 assignments with specific tasks, or a direct answer, a clarifying question or a refusal.
  3. Specialists work in parallel (asyncio.gather), each in a tool loop of up to 4 rounds and 2 source searches, then return a memo: findings, key numbers, source ids and when a professional is worth it.
  4. Huddle: each specialist reads the others' memos and writes one note (under 45 words) to the teammate whose work most affects theirs.
  5. Captain summarizes: 2–4 options with numbers and trade-offs, what the team noticed together, when to hire a professional, cited sources and suggested to-dos. Then the output guard and audit run.

Every step is streamed to the browser as an event (newline-delimited JSON), which is what makes the office panel live. Each run is also stored with its full event log, which is how the traced run below was produced.

Model choice

All agents use gpt-6-luna through the course Portkey key, the model the course allows. Two settings mattered more than the model itself:

  • Reasoning off for tool calls. The provider rejects function tools unless reasoning_effort="none", so specialists run without hidden reasoning. That is also faster and cheaper, and it doesn't hurt accuracy because the hard part (the math) is in code.
  • Low reasoning only where judgment matters. The Captain's plan uses "low" reasoning: deciding whom to involve and whether a request is harmful benefits from a little thinking. Early tests showed reasoning tokens silently eating the output budget and truncating the summary JSON, which is why everything else runs with reasoning off.

6. Safety

Lecture 12's lesson is that models read instructions and data as one stream, and that the model's training is "a soft defense, not a guarantee." The Froggy lab showed white text in a PDF fooling an agent every time. iConcierge reads families' leases, letters and tax forms, so it puts its defenses in code, in four layers (backend/safety.py), and shows each one firing.

8. Limits and next steps

Limits

  • No real-user trial yet, so usefulness for families is untested beyond my own runs.
  • Federal only. The tax estimator covers 2026 federal brackets for single and married-joint filers; no state taxes, credits or head-of-household brackets. Specialists say so in their answers.
  • General information, not licensed advice. The team can be wrong or incomplete; the design leans on sources, shown math and "when to hire a professional," not on authority.
  • Guards are pattern-based. A cleverly paraphrased instruction can slip past the document flagger (the random markers and output guard still apply). The hidden-text check assumes a light page background, and photos rely on the model transcribing only visible text.
  • Local data isn't encrypted at rest. Passcodes are hashed, but the SQLite file holds profiles and documents in plain form. Hosting would need encryption and a privacy review.
  • Profile vs. message conflicts are noticed (run 3) but not resolved automatically.

Next steps

  1. Beta test with 5–10 families (for example through a New Haven housing nonprofit or VITA site): time to a usable answer with and without iConcierge, a comprehension check, and whether each family took a to-do.
  2. State packs: state income tax, tenant law and benefits rules as data files, starting with Connecticut and Florida.
  3. Warm handoffs: turn "when to hire a professional" into a printable summary for a legal-aid office, VITA volunteer or HUD counselor.
  4. Connect Read Me First: let Ava use the legal-paper decoder for court papers and deadlines.
  5. Stronger guards: a small classifier for paraphrased injections, rendered-page OCR comparison for PDFs, and encryption at rest before hosting.
