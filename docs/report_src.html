2. Motivation

Most of life's expensive decisions land on families who have no professional to call. Buying a first home, deciding whether to pay off a credit card or start a Roth IRA, reading a lease before signing it, or choosing life insurance when a baby is coming: each question crosses several fields at once (budget, taxes, contracts, insurance), and each field has its own expert who bills by the hour.

  • Most people go without financial advice. About one in three U.S. adults (34%) works with a financial advisor, versus 74% of millionaires (Northwestern Mutual 2025 Planning & Progress Study, as reported by 24/7 Wall St.).
  • Most legal problems get no help. Low-income Americans received no or not enough legal help for 92% of their substantial civil legal problems (Legal Services Corporation, 2022 Justice Gap Study).
  • Help is expensive. The average U.S. lawyer's hourly rate is $349 (Clio Legal Trends).

The idea grew out of earlier work on housing navigation in New Haven: people in a housing crisis rarely have just one problem. A missed rent payment is also a budget problem, a benefits problem and sometimes a legal problem. A single chatbot answers whatever it's asked. The bet behind iConcierge was that a team would notice that a question about a down payment is also a question about an emergency fund and life insurance. Section 7 tests that bet blind, and it only partly holds.

Goal. Give any household the experience of a family office ("let me get the right people on it") for less than a penny per question, without telling anyone what to do: options, numbers, trade-offs and sources, and the family decides.

Named alternatives and how families use them today. General chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) can run code and search the web, but nothing makes them use a calculator, a dated tax table or a vetted source; in Section 7 the same model without tools used 2025 tax tables and repeated text hidden in a PDF. AI assistants inside single products (Intuit Assist in TurboTax, Cleo for budgeting, DoNotPay for consumer legal tasks) stay within one domain. Single-domain services each cover one slice: TurboTax and H&R Block for taxes, LegalZoom for legal documents, Betterment and Wealthfront for investing, Zillow and NerdWallet calculators for housing and budgets. Directories such as 211, findhelp.org and New Haven Navigator point people to programs. A family with a cross-cutting question (a down payment that is also an emergency-fund, tax and insurance question) has to stitch these together alone. iConcierge's difference is the coordinated team, code-computed numbers, vetted sources and drafts that turn advice into action.

It is deliberately not a "chat with your PDF" tool: documents are one input the specialists can choose to read, not the product. Compared with resource directories such as New Haven Navigator, iConcierge doesn't send people to a list; it works the problem with them and shows its math.

Permissions are enforced in code (tools.TOOLSETS): a specialist that calls another specialist's tool gets an error, not a result. No agent has internet, email or payment tools.

How they are organized

  1. Input guard cleans the message (Section 6).
  2. Captain plans (JSON): acknowledgment, 0–3 assignments with specific tasks, or a direct answer, a clarifying question or a refusal.
  3. Specialists work in parallel (asyncio.gather), each in a tool loop of up to 4 rounds and 2 source searches, then return a memo: findings, key numbers, source ids and when a professional is worth it.
  4. Huddle: each specialist reads the others' memos and writes one note (under 45 words) to the teammate whose work most affects theirs.
  5. Ari drafts (only when the family wants something written): the advisors hand their findings to Ari, who saves email drafts. Every draft passes the output guard before it is stored.
  6. Captain summarizes: 2–4 options with numbers and trade-offs, what the team noticed together, when to hire a professional, cited sources and suggested to-dos. Then the output guard and audit run.

Every step is streamed to the browser as an event (newline-delimited JSON), which is what makes the office panel live. Each run is also stored with its full event log, which is how the traced run below was produced.

Model choice

All agents use gpt-6-luna through the course Portkey key, the model the course allows. Two settings mattered more than the model itself:

  • Reasoning off for tool calls. The provider rejects function tools unless reasoning_effort="none", so specialists run without hidden reasoning. That is also faster and cheaper, and it doesn't hurt accuracy because the hard part (the math) is in code.
  • Low reasoning only where judgment matters. The Captain's plan uses "low" reasoning: deciding whom to involve and whether a request is harmful benefits from a little thinking. Early tests showed reasoning tokens silently eating the output budget and truncating the summary JSON, which is why everything else runs with reasoning off.

6. Safety

Lecture 12's lesson is that models read instructions and data as one stream, and that the model's training is "a soft defense, not a guarantee." The Froggy lab showed white text in a PDF fooling an agent every time. iConcierge reads families' leases, letters and tax forms, so it puts its defenses in code, in four layers (backend/safety.py), and shows each one firing.

Does the team beat one AI? A blind test

The 9 measured questions went through three setups on gpt-6-luna: iConcierge; one agent with every calculator, the source library and the same output guard; and the bare model given the question and the family's details (a stand-in for a general chatbot). A separate AI grader (Claude), blind to which was which, scored all 27 answers 1–5 on correctness, completeness, usefulness, safety/honesty and clarity against reference facts (IRS 2026 figures, the real lease terms). Round 1 exposed three bugs, all fixed: answers now follow the language of the question, the Captain may not do its own arithmetic, and the payoff calculator overstated interest when the last payment is partial. Round 2 was a fresh run with a new grader. Scripts, all 54 answers and the grades are in docs/eval/.

8. Limits and next steps

Limits

  • The team is not better than one agent on answer quality (Section 7), and costs about four times as much.
  • Two real users so far, testing in my presence. Answers can take about 40 s when the provider is busy, and both found them too brief.
  • Federal only. The tax estimator covers 2026 federal brackets for single and married-joint filers; no state taxes, credits or head-of-household brackets. Specialists say so in their answers.
  • General information, not licensed advice. The team can be wrong or incomplete; the design leans on sources, shown math and "when to hire a professional," not on authority.
  • Guards are pattern-based. A paraphrased instruction can slip past the document flagger (the random markers and output guard still apply); the hidden-text check assumes a light page.
  • Local data isn't encrypted at rest (passcodes are hashed). Hosting would need encryption and a privacy review.
  • No live email connection yet. Ari hands drafts off through links and the clipboard.

Next steps

  1. Route by need: one agent with the same tools and guards for most questions; the round table as an opt-in "show me the team" view.
  2. Beta test with 5–10 families (e.g., through a New Haven housing nonprofit or VITA site); the beta switch already logs each run and a 1–5 rating per answer. Show the answer as it is written, so waits feel shorter.
  3. Deeper answers on request: a "Tell me more" button (more searches, longer answer); the default stays cheap.
  4. State packs: state tax, tenant law and benefits rules as data files (Connecticut and Florida first).
  5. Warm handoffs: a printable summary for a legal-aid office, VITA volunteer or HUD counselor.
  6. Connections for Ari: Gmail and Outlook through sign-in (OAuth), saving drafts to the family's inbox, never sending.
  7. Stronger guards: a classifier for paraphrased injections, rendered-page OCR checks for PDFs, and encryption at rest before hosting.
